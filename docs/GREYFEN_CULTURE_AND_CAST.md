# LEGEND — Greyfen Culture, Cast & Regional Story Bible

**Status:** Regional design draft. Existing systems and setting facts are marked **implemented**; new cultural details and cast ideas are **proposed** until approved.  
**Purpose:** Make Greyfen a distinctive community with its own story logic, not an alternate color palette for Ashmere.  
**References:** [Greyfen Expansion Plan](GREYFEN_EXPANSION_PLAN.md), [World & Story Bible](LEGEND_WORLD_BIBLE.md), [Ashmere Character Arcs](ASHMERE_CHARACTER_ARCS.md), [First-Session Story Spine](ASHMERE_STORY_SPINE.md).

## 1. Regional identity

### The promise

Ashmere is a road-facing lantern town. Greyfen is a water-facing working settlement. Ashmere’s daily question is, “What comes along the road, and how do we prepare for it?” Greyfen’s is, “Which path still exists, and how do we know it is safe enough to use?”

Greyfen should be practical, communal, damp, and quietly uneasy. It is not a horror village, and its people are not all suspicious mystics. Most residents are trying to get food to market, keep walkways above the waterline, recover useful salvage, and make tomorrow’s route safer than today’s.

### Established geography and visual identity

- Raised timber walks, stilted homes, low bridges, reed roofs, fishing platforms, peat stores, lantern docks, dark shallow water, and weathered stone footings.
- Palette: marsh green, wet charcoal timber, cold slate, muddy umber, tarnished brass, and small amber lights.
- Key destinations: Reedgate landing, the Silted Cup, Float Market, the Drowned Anvil, Boundary Archive, Work Board, and Marsh Path.
- Signature contract: **A Dry Crossing**, which asks the player to recover a route marker washed loose from a raised path.

Keep the human-made structures central. Greyfen’s beauty comes from how people adapt to a difficult landscape: poles cut to known heights, repair boards stored under roofs, ropes hung where hands can find them in fog, lanterns protected from wind, and route marks that people can check without a map.

## 2. Daily life and local economy

These are proposed cultural details that fit the implemented setting; they are not automatically canon.

### Livelihoods
- **Peat cutting and drying:** Peat is stored above water and covered against rain. A good dry batch matters for cooking and winter heat, not merely as a sellable item.
- **Fishing and dock work:** Nets, poles, boats, line repair, and the maintenance of small piers are everyday labor.
- **Salvage sorting:** Floodwater exposes useful timber, iron pieces, rope, jars, and ruined household goods. Most salvage is mundane, and its value depends on cleaning and sorting it.
- **Path and marker maintenance:** A path marker is a public safety tool. It is worth more than its material because people use it to judge water depth, footing, and direction.
- **Repair craft:** The Drowned Anvil should feel adapted to the wetland—equipment is built for damp, corrosion, rope tension, and walking on narrow boards.

### Community habits
- People notice water levels the way inland towns notice cloud cover. A conversation may begin with “How high was it at first light?” before anyone asks about the traveler.
- Residents use small repairs as social signals. Replacing a loose board, returning a borrowed rope, or resetting a marker is a visible form of care.
- The market’s reputation depends on honest grading of salvage. A basket of mixed scrap and a basket of usable iron pieces are not the same product.
- Good local knowledge is usually specific: the post that leans after heavy rain, the board that swells, the current that turns near a stone footing. Avoid generic lines about “the marsh knowing your name.”

### Sensory palette
- **Visual:** horizontal boardwalks cutting across reflective water; stilt supports; ropes; peat stacks; brass bells or marker plates; pale waterlines on stone.
- **Sound:** soft water against timber, rope creak, boardwalk footfalls, distant work calls, tools on wet wood, a warning bell muffled by fog.
- **Smell:** damp timber, peat smoke, river mud, fish brine, rain on old stone.
- **Warmth:** amber lamps in windows, kettle steam, the Silted Cup’s crowded boards, dry gloves hung close to a stove.

Greyfen can feel uncanny without making every reed glow blue. Keep the supernatural rare enough that the player notices when an ordinary route behaves unusually.

## 3. Local language and customs

Use specific, practical vocabulary sparingly. The goal is to make locals sound like they live here, not to create a glossary the player must memorize.

**Candidate sayings (proposed):**
- “Count the posts before you cross.” — check the route, then commit.
- “Water takes the shortest word first.” — warnings must be simple and clear.
- “A dry board is a promise.” — a repaired path is a commitment to neighbors.
- “Mark it twice if the fog has risen.” — verify route information when conditions are poor.
- “The water keeps what the ledger misses.” — a practical saying about lost property and incomplete records; it need not refer to the central mystery.

**Customs worth testing in dialogue:**
- Public route markers have a recognizable local notch or plate so an altered marker can be spotted.
- Residents share a waterline mark or crossing status before a major market day.
- A recovered route marker is cleaned and recorded before it is installed. That makes the **A Dry Crossing** objective feel civic rather than arbitrary.
- A traveler who returns with useful route information is asked what they observed, not merely whether they survived.

Do not make all locals speak in proverbs. Ordinary, direct conversation is the baseline; customs surface when the situation calls for them.

## 4. The Bellkeeper question

**Current implementation:** Greyfen uses the names **Bellkeeper’s Hall** and **Bellkeeper’s Ledger** for work/record-related spaces or roles. This is a meaningful name that needs a deliberate definition.

**Recommended separation (proposed):** In everyday Greyfen life, bellkeeping is a practical civic responsibility. Bells signal water conditions, market opening, a closed causeway, or a call for help when visibility is poor. They are tools maintained by people who need timely warnings.

The **bell that rang once** near the traveler remains a separate mystery. A familiar sound can make that event more unsettling, but the game should not imply that Greyfen’s working warning bells caused it unless later evidence establishes that connection.

Questions to resolve before writing the Bellkeeper as a major character:
1. Is “Bellkeeper” an office, a person, or a historic institution?
2. Does the Bellkeeper’s Ledger track local warnings, route changes, missing travelers, or all three?
3. Are Greyfen’s bells ordinary devices whose sound becomes suspicious only in context, or do locals already know of older bell stories?
4. What would be the consequence if a bell sounded at the wrong tide, time, or location?

Keep the answer small in the first Greyfen chapter. A practical function first; an unanswered question second.

## 5. Greyfen’s cast: design roles before locking names

**Continuity rule:** The current source reuses Mara Vell, Old Brenn, and Captain Oric in Greyfen with changed local titles. Decide whether those characters travel with the protagonist or whether Greyfen needs its own keepers. The strongest long-term regional identity is likely served by a local cast, but the decision belongs in the canon ledger before implementation.

Until that choice is made, use role cards rather than permanent names:

### Reedgate Warden — route safety
**Implemented function:** The signature contract starts with a request to recover a washed route marker.

**Proposed personality:** Direct, observant, and busy. The warden judges information by whether it helps someone cross safely, not by how mysterious it sounds.

**Story need:** Get a usable marker back into service before the next market crossing.

**Blind spot:** May overvalue a known route and distrust an unfamiliar solution, even when water levels have changed.

**Gameplay connection:** Explains the two approaches to **A Dry Crossing** and describes the outcomes accurately before the player commits.

### Boundary Archive keeper — route memory
**Implemented function:** Greyfen has a Boundary Archive and region-specific talk about records and bell-marks.

**Proposed personality:** Practical archivist, interested in what changed between two markings rather than what a record supposedly predicts.

**Story need:** Keep a usable history of water levels and altered routes.

**Blind spot:** May trust a recorded waterline long after local conditions have changed.

**Gameplay connection:** Journals, route markers, old seals, recovered fragments, and later comparisons with road evidence.

### Salvage sorter / Float Market regular — value and labor
**Proposed role:** A recurring market character who distinguishes useful material from rubbish, knows who can repair a damaged item, and can explain why a seemingly common resource matters locally.

**Story need:** Keep salvage honest, useful, and distributed fairly.

**Blind spot:** May assume all unfamiliar finds are just another material to sort.

**Gameplay connection:** Trading, work contracts, material sources and prices, and possible recipes. This character should not become a second lore keeper.

### The Drowned Anvil smith
**Proposed role:** A smith who builds and maintains equipment for damp, slippery, and corrosive conditions.

**Story need:** Keep tools functional despite moisture, salt, and limited dry storage.

**Voice:** Describes equipment through use—grip, rust, balance, repair, and failure points.

**Gameplay connection:** Equipment comparisons should show actual combat or travel effects. The smith’s identity is grounded craft, not a secret magical forge.

These are role proposals, not approved names or final biographies. A local cast should get final names, portraits, voice rules, relationships, and save IDs together—not piecemeal.

## 6. Candidate Greyfen residents (proposal only)

These names and personality directions are **concepts for review, not canon**. If the project owner chooses to keep the same three keepers in both towns, discard this roster rather than creating duplicate versions of Mara, Brenn, or Oric.

| Candidate | Role | Immediate want | Voice and friction | Gameplay/story function |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Tamsin Rill** | Reedgate Warden | Reopen a crossing before people depend on it for the next market run | Direct, alert, lightly dry; prefers a workable answer over a long debate | Gives the player the A Dry Crossing contract and explains the route-marker's practical value |
| **Hesta Wren** | Boundary Archive keeper | Keep flood marks and route records usable when old measurements no longer match current conditions | Precise and patient; uneasy about uncertainty but refuses to conceal gaps | Lets the player compare old waterlines and learns to record contradictions without inventing an explanation |
| **Noll Fenn** | Float Market salvage grader | Keep salvage prices credible so residents know what is worth carrying back from the water | Wry, observant, suspicious of grand claims; knows the difference between junk and something that can be repaired | Teaches salvage sorting, market value, and the practical source of Greyfen materials |
| **Nerin Holt** | Drowned Anvil smith | Keep tools and equipment serviceable despite damp, corrosion, and narrow wet walkways | Grounded, craft-proud, economical with words; judges kit by what it can survive | Gives region-specific gear advice and crafting context without turning the smithy into a lore terminal |

### Relationship seeds (proposed)
- Tamsin trusts Hesta’s records but feels the archive can delay practical repairs while it seeks certainty.
- Hesta knows Tamsin’s urgency is justified but worries that a poorly marked route will hurt someone later.
- Noll believes both undercount the labor hidden inside salvaged materials; he can be wrong about a specific item without being dishonest.
- Nerin’s equipment advice can settle a disagreement by showing what damp and corrosion actually do to a tool.

These relationships should appear in small cooperative moments, not a mandatory four-way argument. Their goals overlap—keep the crossing usable and the town supplied—but each notices a different kind of failure.

### Name review rule
Before finalizing any name, check it against Ashmere residents, player origins/keepsakes, existing quest names, and any future region plans. Once chosen, record pronunciation, spelling, portrait silhouette, clothing/material cues, role, first appearance, dialogue ID, and the state that tracks whether the player has met them.

## 7. A Dry Crossing — playable regional story

**Implemented mechanics from the expansion plan:**
- The traveler must recover a washed route marker.
- The safer, slower route follows old posts and guarantees recovery for **35 gold and one Road Herb**.
- The riskier reed-bed shortcut has a **65% recovery chance** and awards **65 gold and two Road Herbs** on success.
- A failed shortcut costs **5 HP** and leaves the contract open.
- Successful recovery is saved and cannot be claimed a second time.

### Story beat structure

**Beat 1 — Explain why the marker matters.** The warden points to the gap in the route, explains who uses that path, and makes it clear that the marker helps ordinary travelers judge where the raised boards end. Do not make the player guess why a stick in the mud matters.

**Beat 2 — Show both approaches honestly.** The old posts take longer but are reliable; the reed-bed shortcut is faster and carries a disclosed chance of failure. The interface must present risk and reward before commitment, not reveal the probability afterward.

**Beat 3 — Let the result change the scene.**
- On safe recovery, the warden checks the marker’s notch/plate, records the route, and pays the guaranteed reward.
- On shortcut success, the marker returns quickly but muddy; a local detail can show how the player’s risk paid off.
- On failure, the traveler returns injured or empty-handed; the task remains open, the missing marker remains a real problem, and the player can try another time.

**Beat 4 — Add a small regional echo.** The next time the player visits the crossing, the dialogue and route status should reflect the recorded result. Do not add a new currency or a separate reputation system if the current quest/flag model is sufficient.

### Why this story belongs in Greyfen

The stakes are civic: a restored marker protects work, trade, and people traveling across water. It shows a different kind of heroism from fighting an enemy on the Old Road. It also gives the player a reason to respect route knowledge without implying that every flood or missing sign is supernatural.

## 7. Local jobs as small stories

The implemented Greyfen Work Board includes jobs for dry peat, salvage sorting, and resetting flood markers. Each should teach a little about the community through the activity itself.

- **Dry Peat Bricks:** Show that storage and weather matter. The item has a clear source, a sell value, and a distinct name.
- **Sort a Salvage Basket:** Make the distinction between usable salvage and scrap legible. Avoid a random reward with no material explanation.
- **Reset Flood Markers:** Make route knowledge a shared responsibility. Reward accuracy, not hidden lore guesses.

For every job, the UI should explain the action, cost/check if any, reward, and failure result. When an item is introduced, its source, sell value, use, and fallback name/icon must be defined.

## 8. How Greyfen contributes to the larger mystery

**Established connection:** Greyfen’s route has changed after the water rose; old markers and records are important to local life. The central Bell/lantern mystery remains open.

**Proposed through-line:** The player can compare a record from Greyfen with a mark seen on the Old Road and discover that the same shape appears in two different practical contexts. It should raise a question about route memory or recordkeeping, not prove a cosmology.

Do not turn every local detail into a clue. Some jobs are simply work; some locals are simply hungry, tired, proud, or funny. The larger mystery becomes more powerful when normal life remains real around it.

- [ ] The Bellkeeper’s Hall has a clear practical function before any supernatural meaning is attached.
- [ ] A Dry Crossing communicates its risk/reward correctly and preserves saved completion state.
- [ ] A failed shortcut does not silently finish the task or duplicate rewards.
- [ ] Work rewards match inventory data, item definitions, and market values.
- [ ] Local speech and sounds evoke wetland life without making everyone cryptic.
- [ ] A local problem has human consequences independent of the main mystery.
